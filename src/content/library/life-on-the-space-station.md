## A Home Above the Clouds

Right now, as I speak, there are people living and working high above our heads. They are on the International Space Station, a large laboratory that circles the Earth about four hundred kilometres up. It has had people on board without a break since the year two thousand. Today, I want to take you on a short tour of daily life up there, because it is both amazing and surprisingly ordinary.

## Sixteen Sunrises a Day

The station moves very fast, at around twenty-eight thousand kilometres per hour. At that speed, it goes all the way around the Earth in about ninety minutes. That means the crew can see around sixteen sunrises and sixteen sunsets every single day.

Imagine trying to sleep with the Sun rising every hour and a half. To keep a normal routine, astronauts follow a fixed schedule based on clock time, not on the light outside the window. They wake up, work, exercise, eat and sleep at set hours, just as we do.

## Floating Through the Day

Everything on the station floats. Astronauts do not walk from room to room. They gently push off a wall and glide. A pen that is not tied down can drift away and get lost. So many things are fixed in place with straps, clips and pieces of tape that grip.

Sleeping is a good example. There is no up or down, so astronauts sleep in small sleeping bags attached to the wall of a tiny cabin. Some say it is one of the most comfortable beds they have ever had, because there is no pressure on any part of the body.

## Eating and Drinking in Space

Food is carefully planned. Much of it comes packed in pouches, and some of it is dried, so the crew adds water before eating. Crumbs are a real problem, because they float and can get into equipment, or even into someone's eyes. So foods like crumbly bread are avoided, and flat breads such as tortillas are popular instead.

Water is precious, so the station recycles as much of it as possible, including moisture from the air. Cleaned and treated, that water is used again. It sounds strange, but it is a smart way to live with very limited supplies.

## Staying Strong

Living without gravity is hard on the human body. On Earth, our muscles and bones work all day just to hold us up. In space, they hardly need to work, so they slowly become weaker. To fight this, astronauts exercise for around two hours every day. They use special machines, like a treadmill with straps that hold them down, and equipment that lets them lift heavy loads.

Even so, coming back to Earth after a long mission takes time. Astronauts often need several weeks to feel fully steady on their feet again.

Mind matters as much as body. Living in a small space with the same few people for months is not easy, so the crew stays in touch with family through calls and messages. In their free time, many astronauts simply float by the window and watch the Earth go by.

## Why They Are There

So what do they actually do all day? Mostly, they do science. The station is a laboratory where scientists can study things that are hard to test on Earth, like how plants grow without gravity, how fire behaves, and how the human body changes over long periods in space. What we learn helps prepare for longer journeys, perhaps one day to the Moon and Mars. The crew also repairs equipment and keeps the station running.

## A Shared Achievement

The International Space Station was built piece by piece in orbit, by several countries working together. It is one of the most complex things humans have ever made. And yet, life on board comes down to simple human things: sleeping, eating, staying healthy and doing good work with your team.

The next time you see a bright, steady light moving quickly across the night sky, it might just be the station, with people inside it, looking back down at us. Thank you.
