## A Message Across the World

Imagine you are sitting at home, and you send a photo to a friend who lives in another country. A few seconds later, your friend is looking at it. We do this every day, and we almost never stop to think about it. But how does that photo actually travel? Today, I want to explain how the internet works, in the simplest way I can.

## A Network of Networks

Let us start with the word itself. The internet is a network of networks. Your phone, your laptop and your television at home are connected to each other through a small box called a router. That is one small network. Your internet company connects your home to thousands of other homes. That is a bigger network. And these companies connect to each other across cities, countries and oceans. Put all of these together, and you get the internet.

So the internet is not a cloud floating in the sky. It is very physical. It is made of cables, towers and large buildings full of computers. In fact, a large part of the world's internet traffic travels through long cables that lie on the floor of the ocean.

## Addresses and Names

Now, when you post a letter, you need an address. The internet works the same way. Every device that is connected gets a number called an IP address. This number tells the network where to deliver the information.

But numbers are hard for people to remember. So we use names instead, like the name of a website. When you type that name, your device quietly asks a special service, "What is the number for this name?" This service is called DNS, the Domain Name System. You can think of it as the phone book of the internet. You know the name, and it gives you the number.

## Breaking It Into Pieces

Here is the part I find most interesting. Your photo does not travel as one big piece. It is broken into many small pieces called packets. Each packet carries a little bit of the photo, along with the address it is going to.

Think of it like sending a large jigsaw puzzle by post. Instead of one giant box, you send each piece in its own small envelope. The envelopes may take different roads. Some may arrive earlier, and some may arrive later. But every envelope has a number on it. So at the other end, your friend's phone puts all the pieces back in the right order. And if one piece is lost on the way, the phone simply asks for it again.

Along the way, special machines called routers act like traffic police at a junction. Each one looks at the address on a packet and points it toward the next stop. All of this happens in a fraction of a second.

## Servers and Websites

So what happens when you open a website? A website lives on a computer called a server. A server is just a computer that is always switched on and always ready to answer. When you open a page, your device sends a request to that server. It is like saying, "Please send me this page." The server replies with the text, the pictures and the videos. Your browser then puts them together and shows you the page.

It is a little like ordering food at a restaurant. You are the customer. You ask for a dish. The kitchen prepares it and sends it to your table.

## Bringing It Together

Let me put it all together. The internet is a huge network of connected devices. Every device has an address. Information is broken into small packets. Routers guide those packets across the world. And servers answer our requests, day and night.

The next time you send a message or watch a video, pause for a second. Think about the journey it has just made, through cables under the sea, in about the time it takes to blink. It is one of the most remarkable things people have ever built, and we carry it in our pockets. Thank you.
